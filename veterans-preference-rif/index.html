Veterans’ Preference in Federal Reductions in Force

An Interactive Guide to the 2026 Reduction-in-Force Rule

Agard Research Associates Inc.

Veterans’ Preference in Federal Reductions in Force is an interactive guide to the reduction-in-force rule that the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) published at 91 Fed. Reg. 49,178 and that took effect on September 2, 2026. The rule changes the order in which federal employees are kept or released in a layoff. Veterans’ preference once placed a veteran rated acceptable ahead of every nonveteran in the same tenure group; it is now a fixed number of points added to a score built from performance ratings. The guide explains the change, ranks sample employees under the prior rules and the final rule, and shows how the value of the veterans’ credit depends on how agencies rate their employees. It draws on the ARA policy analysis of the same title.

This resource provides general information only and does not constitute legal advice. Agard Research Associates Inc. is nonpartisan and does not support or oppose any political party, candidate, or legislation. The sample register and the ratings model are illustrations and do not determine any employee’s retention standing.

Interactive Guide

The bottom line

Veterans’ preference in a federal reduction in force (RIF) used to be a place in line. Under the rule in effect since September 2, 2026, it is a fixed number of points that a nonveteran with higher ratings can overcome. Employees are now ranked mainly by their three most recent ratings of record, on a scale that runs to 21 points. Veterans with a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more add 5 points, and other preference eligibles add 3 (5 C.F.R. § 351.504(a)).

The rule governs RIF notices issued on or after September 2, 2026. Whether it is consistent with 5 U.S.C. § 3502 is disputed, and as of November 1, 2026, no lawsuit directly challenging it had been identified.

1.What the Rule Changes

In a RIF, employees who compete for the same kinds of positions are listed on a retention register, and those with the lowest standing are the first to be separated, demoted, or furloughed. Federal law directs OPM to give “due effect” to tenure, military preference, length of service, and performance in setting that order (5 U.S.C. § 3502(a)). Under OPM’s prior regulations, performance mattered only among employees in the same tenure group and veterans’ preference subgroup, so preference decided every comparison between a veteran and a nonveteran in the same tenure group (5 C.F.R. § 351.501(a)-(c) (2025)). The final rule puts a single score first and makes preference part of that score. In this guide, “veteran” means an employee with veterans’ preference.

2.How the Score Is Calculated

Each employee’s score is the sum of the points for three ratings of record, plus the veterans’ credit where it applies.

Table 2. Points under the final rule

Rating of record or preference statusPoints
Level 5, Outstanding7
Level 4, Exceeds Fully Successful5
Level 3, Fully Successful3
Level 2 or Level 10
Preference eligible with a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more (AD)+5
Other preference eligible (A)+3

Sources: 5 C.F.R. §§ 351.503(a), 351.504(a); 91 Fed. Reg. at 49,220-22.

Which ratings count. The score uses the three most recent ratings of record received in the four years before RIF notices issue. One rating is counted three times, two ratings are averaged to supply the third, and an employee with no rating receives the modal rating (5 C.F.R. § 351.503; 91 Fed. Reg. at 49,220-22).

How ties are broken. Employees with equal scores are ordered by tenure subgroup and then by service computation date (5 C.F.R. § 351.501(c)).

When standing is fixed. Retention standing is determined as of the date an employee receives a specific RIF notice, not the date the RIF takes effect (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,178-79).

What the credit is worth. OPM calculates the 3- and 5-point credits as 14.3 and 23.8 percent of the 21-point scale (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,181). Under Pattern C, adjacent rating levels from Fully Successful to Outstanding differ by 2 points per rating, so a one-level difference sustained across all three ratings is worth 6 points, more than either credit. Under Pattern B, which has no Level 4, a single step from Fully Successful to Outstanding is worth 4 points, more than the 3-point credit.

Outcomes under the prior rules assume both employees are in the same tenure group. Ties, which occur in about 1 percent of comparisons or fewer, are counted as not ranking ahead.

Rating spread. Under Pattern C, the credit counts for most when ratings cluster at Fully Successful and for least when they spread across the top levels, so OPM’s standardized distribution and cap raise the practical value of preference, other things equal. Pattern B is the exception: because one step up is worth 4 points, the 3-point credit gives a typical veteran the lowest chance of ranking ahead in Scenario D.

Persistent ratings. If employees receive the same rating every year, a Fully Successful veteran never outranks a nonveteran with consistent Level 4 ratings, whatever the credit.

Timing. Because the score draws on a four-year window, RIFs conducted in 2026 through 2028 will rely partly on ratings issued before the cap took effect. For those RIFs, Scenario A is the closest benchmark, and it is the scenario in which the credit protects Fully Successful veterans least.

5.Who Is Affected

RIFs were rare until 2025. The Partnership for Public Service reports that RIF separations never exceeded 300 in a fiscal year during the preceding decade but that 10,436 employees were separated by RIF between January 20, 2025, and January 2026 (Partnership for Public Service 2026b).

621,751Veterans in the executive branch, 27.4 percent of 2,267,091 employees. Of these, 545,352 had veterans’ preference.
262,385Veterans with a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more, 11.6 percent of all employees and 42.2 percent of veterans. They receive the 5-point credit.
314,311Veterans at the Department of Defense (DoD), 43.0 percent of its workforce and about half of all veterans in the executive branch.
122,094Veterans at the Department of Veterans Affairs, more than any agency other than DoD and 25.3 percent of its workforce.

Source: OPM 2026b, i, 4-6.

Veterans outside DoD. DoD has ranked employees by rating of record, tenure group, average score, veterans’ preference, and service computation date since January 2017, so preference there already works mainly as a tie-breaker after ratings (DoD 2021, paras. 1.2.a, 3.3.c, 3.4; 91 Fed. Reg. at 49,188). The change in method falls chiefly on the roughly 307,000 veterans at other agencies.

Long-serving veterans. Among veterans who still compete, those with long federal careers lose the most, because length of service, which compounded their preference under the prior rules, is now only a tie-breaker.

Veterans on probation. A veteran serving probation once competed in Group II with preference. Under the final rule, that veteran is not a competing employee (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,178-79), and whether the change helps or hurts depends on how the agency uses its discretion.

Career-conditional veterans. Veterans who have completed probation but not the three years of service needed for career tenure no longer sit below every career nonveteran and can outrank one with weaker ratings (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,183).

6.Both Sides of the Question

The central legal question is whether a fixed credit that a higher-rated nonveteran can overcome satisfies the statute’s command that a qualifying preference eligible “is entitled to be retained in preference to other competing employees” (5 U.S.C. § 3502(c)).

Supporters, including OPM, the Office of Special Counsel, the Departments of Agriculture and Labor, FGA Action, and, with conditions, the Niskanen Center, say performance should count for more in RIFs. FGA Action argued that performance has been “systematically underweighted” (FGA Action 2026, 2), and the Niskanen Center said the old order released newer employees with the most marketable skills first (Niskanen Center 2026, 4-5). OPM reads § 3502 as granting “a meaningful retention preference, not a categorical right to be retained ahead of every non-veteran regardless of performance,” and calls its credit “materially stronger than hiring preference as a scoring weight” (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,181-83).

Critics, including federal employee unions and the National Employment Lawyers Association, say the statute protects any veteran rated acceptable (AFGE and AFSCME 2026, 2; NELA 2026, 1-2), a reading that draws support from Hilton v. Sullivan, 334 U.S. 323 (1948), and from Congress’s rejection in 1978 of a proposed time limit on retention preference. Disabled American Veterans argued that the “due effect” mandate requires a balance “ensuring that no single factor dominates,” called preference “a substantive statutory entitlement,” and warned it would become “a largely illusory consideration” (DAV 2026b, 2-3); OPM called that contention “not accurate” (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,182). The Veterans of Foreign Wars supported modernization but warned against making preference “a secondary, point-based consideration” and asked OPM to keep it “meaningful and enforceable” (VFW 2026, 1-2).

The research record carries a caution for both sides. GAO found that employees without preference were about two to seven times as likely to lose their jobs at three military installations in fiscal year 1991 (GAO 1998, 1), and that women and minorities were disproportionately separated at three DoD installations, in part because they ranked lower on retention factors, including veterans’ preference (GAO 1996, 2). In the report’s reading, the categorical preference protected veterans substantially and shifted RIF losses toward nonveterans, while a weighted preference spreads losses more evenly but makes outcomes depend on the accuracy of performance ratings.

ARA presents both sides and takes no position on whether the rule is wise or lawful.

Indicators ARA will monitor

Litigation over the rule. A facial challenge to the rule or the appeals rule, or a VEOA appeal raising § 3502(c), would test the central legal question and the reach of the bar on judicial review.

Rating distributions. OPM’s fiscal year 2026 results, the cap it sets for fiscal year 2027, and agencies’ choice of summary patterns will set the practical value of the credit.

RIF volume and composition. Data on separations by preference status would show whether the modeled effects appear in practice.

DoD alignment. Whether DoD revises its instruction to conform to the new part 351 will determine how its roughly 314,000 veterans are ranked.

Congressional action. Appropriations riders or legislation on retention preference or RIF appeal rights would change the legal baseline.

OPM appeal decisions. The first OPM decisions in RIF appeals brought by preference eligibles will show how OPM applies § 3502(c).

8.If You Have Veterans’ Preference

Check your ratings of record. Confirm that all of your ratings from the past four years appear in your personnel records. Missing ratings are filled in by formula, and your three most recent ratings now set most of your standing.

Check your preference status. Confirm that your records show the correct veterans’ preference. Only a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more earns the 5-point credit; other preference eligibles receive 3.

Know where appeals go. For RIF notices issued on or after September 2, 2026, appeals go to OPM rather than the Merit Systems Protection Board, and OPM’s rule bars grievances over RIFs and court review of its decisions (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,230, 49,242-43).

Know the complaint deadline. Under the Veterans Employment Opportunities Act, a preference eligible who believes a RIF violated a veterans’ preference law or regulation may file a complaint with the Department of Labor’s Veterans’ Employment and Training Service within 60 days after the alleged violation, pursue it before the Merit Systems Protection Board, and in some cases take it to federal district court (5 U.S.C. §§ 3330a(a), 3330b).
