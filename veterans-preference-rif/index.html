Veterans’ Preference in Federal Reductions in Force

An Interactive Guide to the 2026 Reduction-in-Force Rule

Agard Research Associates Inc.

Veterans’ Preference in Federal Reductions in Force is an interactive guide to the reduction-in-force rule that the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) published at 91 Fed. Reg. 49,178 and that took effect on September 2, 2026. The rule changes the order in which federal employees are kept or released in a layoff. Veterans’ preference once placed a veteran rated acceptable ahead of every nonveteran in the same tenure group; it is now a fixed number of points added to a score built from performance ratings. The guide explains the change, ranks sample employees under the prior rules and the final rule, and shows how the value of the veterans’ credit depends on how agencies rate their employees. It draws on the ARA policy analysis of the same title.

This resource provides general information only and does not constitute legal advice. Agard Research Associates Inc. is nonpartisan and does not support or oppose any political party, candidate, or legislation. The sample register and the ratings model are illustrations and do not determine any employee’s retention standing.

Interactive Guide

The bottom line

In a federal reduction in force (RIF), veterans’ preference used to be a place in line. Under the rule in effect since September 2, 2026, it is a fixed number of points that a nonveteran with higher ratings can overcome. Employees are now ranked mainly by their three most recent ratings of record, on a scale whose ordinary maximum is 21 points. Preference eligibles with a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more add 5 points, and other preference eligibles add 3 (5 C.F.R. § 351.504(a)).

The rule governs RIF notices issued on or after September 2, 2026. Whether it is consistent with 5 U.S.C. § 3502 is disputed. As of November 1, 2026, the sources reviewed for this guide identify no lawsuit directly challenging it.

1.What the Rule Changes

When an agency must release employees from their positions, it ranks those who compete for the same kinds of jobs on a retention register, and those with the lowest standing are released first. Federal law directs the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) to give “due effect” to tenure of employment, military preference, length of service, and performance ratings in setting that order (5 U.S.C. § 3502(a)). The final rule changes how OPM does so. In this guide, “veteran” means an employee with veterans’ preference.

Before September 2, 2026Prior rules
  1. Tenure groupGroup I (career employees not serving probation) before Group II (career-conditional employees and employees serving probation) before Group III.
  2. Veterans’ preference subgroupAD (compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more), then A (other preference eligibles), then B (all others).
  3. Length of serviceYears of service, plus additional years for up to three recent ratings: 20, 16, or 12 for each rating at Level 5, 4, or 3, added together, divided by 3, and rounded up to a whole number.
From September 2, 2026Final rule
  1. One scorePoints for the three most recent ratings of record, ordinarily up to 21, plus 5 or 3 points for preference eligibles.
  2. Tenure subgroupSubgroup I (career) before subgroup II (career-conditional), used only to break a tie in score.
  3. Service computation dateUsed only when both the score and the tenure subgroup are tied.

Sources: 85 Fed. Reg. at 81,841 (prior tenure groups); 91 Fed. Reg. at 10,907, 10,911-12 (prior order of retention and additional service credit); 5 C.F.R. §§ 351.501(c), 351.503, 351.504(a).

Under the prior rules, preference decided every comparison between a veteran and a nonveteran in the same tenure group. Under the final rule, preference competes with performance on a common scale.

Key terms

Reduction in force
The procedure an agency follows when it releases a competing employee from a competitive level by furlough for more than 30 days, separation, demotion, or reassignment requiring displacement, when the release is required because of lack of work, shortage of funds, insufficient personnel ceiling, reorganization, or the exercise of reemployment or restoration rights (5 C.F.R. § 351.201(a)(2)).
Competitive area and competitive level
The competitive area sets the organizational and geographic limits within which employees compete. The competitive level groups positions in the same grade (or occupational level) and classification series that are similar enough in duties, qualification requirements, pay schedules, and working conditions that an agency could reassign an employee among them without undue interruption (5 C.F.R. §§ 351.402, 351.403(a)(1)).
Retention register
The list on which an agency ranks the competing employees in a competitive level by retention standing (5 C.F.R. § 351.404).
Preference eligible
A veteran who meets the definitions in 5 U.S.C. § 2108, and in some circumstances a veteran’s spouse, unmarried widow or widower, or parent.
Subgroups AD, A, and B
AD covers preference eligibles with a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more, A covers other preference eligibles, and B covers employees without preference. Under the final rule, the labels identify the 5-, 3-, and 0-point credits (5 C.F.R. § 351.504(a)).
Rating of record
The performance rating prepared at the end of an appraisal period, with a summary level such as Level 3, Fully Successful (5 C.F.R. § 430.203).
Modal rating
The summary rating level assigned most often among the actual ratings of record given under the employee’s summary level pattern, within the competitive area (or, at the agency’s option, a larger part of the agency or the whole agency), for the most recently completed appraisal period. It stands in for an employee with no rating (5 C.F.R. §§ 351.203, 351.503).
VEOA complaint
Under the Veterans Employment Opportunities Act of 1998, a preference eligible who believes a veterans’ preference law or regulation was violated may file a complaint with the Secretary of Labor (5 U.S.C. § 3330a).

2.How the Score Is Calculated

Each employee’s score is the sum of the points for three ratings of record, plus the veterans’ credit where it applies.

Table 2. Points under the final rule

Rating of record or preference statusPoints
Level 5, Outstanding7
Level 4, Exceeds Fully Successful5
Level 3, Fully Successful3
Level 2 or Level 10
Compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more (AD)+5
Other preference eligible (A)+3

Sources: 5 C.F.R. §§ 351.503(a), 351.504(a); 91 Fed. Reg. at 49,220-22.

A worked comparison

Rating 1 + Rating 2 + Rating 3 + veterans’ credit

Veteran with a 30 percent disability, rated 3, 3, and 3: 3 + 3 + 3 + 514
Nonveteran rated 4, 4, and 4: 5 + 5 + 515

The nonveteran ranks ahead. A one-level difference held across all three ratings is worth 6 points, more than either credit.

Which ratings count. The score uses the three most recent ratings of record received in the four years before RIF notices issue. One rating is counted three times, two ratings are averaged to supply the third, and an employee with no rating receives the modal rating (5 C.F.R. § 351.503; 91 Fed. Reg. at 49,220-22).

How ties are broken. Employees with equal scores are ordered by tenure subgroup and then by service computation date (5 C.F.R. § 351.501(c)).

When standing is fixed. Retention standing is determined as of the date an employee receives a specific RIF notice, not the date the RIF takes effect (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,178-79).

What the credit is worth on the scale. OPM calculates the 3- and 5-point credits as 14.3 and 23.8 percent of the 21-point scale (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,181). Under Pattern C, adjacent rating levels from Fully Successful to Outstanding differ by 2 points per rating. Under Pattern B, which has no Level 4, a single step from Fully Successful to Outstanding is worth 4 points, more than the 3-point credit.

      Rating spread. Under Pattern C, the credit counts for most when ratings cluster at Fully Successful and for least when they spread across the top levels, so OPM’s standardized distribution and cap raise the practical value of preference, other things equal. Pattern B is the exception: because one step up is worth 4 points, the 3-point credit gives a typical veteran the lowest chance of ranking ahead in Scenario D.

      Persistent ratings. If employees receive the same rating every year, a Fully Successful veteran never outranks a nonveteran with consistent Level 4 ratings, whatever the credit.

      Timing. Because the score draws on a four-year window, RIFs conducted in 2026 through 2028 will rely partly on ratings issued before the cap took effect. In ARA’s judgment, the more a register depends on those earlier ratings, the better Scenario A serves as a benchmark, and it is the scenario in which the credit protects Fully Successful veterans least.

      5.Who Is Affected

      RIFs were rare until 2025. The Partnership for Public Service reports that RIF separations never exceeded 300 in a fiscal year during the preceding decade but that 10,436 employees were separated by RIF between January 20, 2025, and January 2026.

      Change is largestVeterans outside DoD

      DoD has ranked employees by rating of record, tenure group, average score, veterans’ preference, and service computation date since January 2017 (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,188), so preference there already works mainly as a tie-breaker after ratings (DoD 2021, paras. 1.2.a, 3.3.c, 3.4). The change in method falls chiefly on the roughly 267,000 preference-eligible veterans at other agencies (OPM 2026b, 19).

      Lose the most among those rankedLong-serving veterans

      Length of service, which compounded their preference under the prior rules, is now only a tie-breaker, so a few rating points can place a newer nonveteran ahead.

      Removed from competitionVeterans on probation

      A veteran serving an initial probationary period is no longer a competing employee (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,178-79). Whether that helps or hurts depends on how the agency uses its discretion to keep or release probationers.

      Can gainCareer-conditional veterans

      Veterans who have completed probation but are not yet career employees (typically, OPM notes, those with more than 3 years of creditable service; 91 Fed. Reg. at 10,907) no longer sit below every career nonveteran and can outrank one with weaker ratings (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,183).

      6.Both Sides of the Question

      The central legal question is whether a fixed credit that a higher-rated nonveteran can overcome satisfies the statute’s command that a qualifying preference eligible “is entitled to be retained in preference to other competing employees” (5 U.S.C. § 3502(c)).

      Supporters
      Performance should count for more

      OPM supported the change, as did the Office of Special Counsel (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,184), the Departments of Agriculture and Labor (USDA 2026, 1; DOL 2026, 4), and FGA Action. The Niskanen Center supported it with conditions (Niskanen Center 2026, 4-5). FGA Action argued that performance has been “systematically underweighted” (FGA Action 2026, 2). The Niskanen Center said that because the old rules retain staff by tenure, length of service, and veterans’ preference, RIFs are “often disproportionately targeted at newer employees that have the most marketable skills” (Niskanen Center 2026, 4). OPM reads the statute as granting “a meaningful retention preference, not a categorical right to be retained ahead of every non-veteran regardless of performance” (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,182), and calls its credit

      “materially stronger than hiring preference as a scoring weight” (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,181).

      Critics
      Preference is an entitlement

      Federal employee unions and the National Employment Lawyers Association argue that the statute protects any veteran whose performance is acceptable (AFGE and AFSCME 2026, 2; NELA 2026, 1-2), a reading that draws support from Hilton v. Sullivan, 334 U.S. 323 (1948), which construed the retention preference in section 12 of the Veterans’ Preference Act of 1944. Disabled American Veterans said the statute’s four factors reflect “a deliberate balance,” “ensuring that no single factor dominates to the detriment of the others,” and called preference

      “a substantive statutory entitlement” (DAV 2026b, 2).

      DAV warned that the rule would reduce preference to “a largely illusory consideration” (DAV 2026b, 3), a characterization OPM called “not accurate” (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,182). The Veterans of Foreign Wars supported modernization but warned against making preference “a secondary, point-based consideration” (VFW 2026, 1-2).

      Research record
      A caution for both sides

      GAO found that employees without preference were about two to seven times as likely to lose their jobs at three military installations in fiscal year 1991 (GAO 1998, 1). Reviewing RIFs at three DoD installations, it also found that women and minorities were disproportionately separated, “in part because they ranked lower than white males in one of three retention factors, including veterans’ preference” (GAO 1996, 2).

      In the report’s reading, the categorical preference protected veterans substantially and shifted RIF losses toward nonveterans, while a weighted preference spreads losses more evenly but makes outcomes depend on the accuracy of performance ratings.

      ARA presents both sides and takes no position on whether the rule is wise or lawful.

      7.Where Things Stand

      Status as of November 1, 2026.

      1. December 17, 2020OPM proposes ranking employees by performance within each tenure group, ahead of veterans’ preference (85 Fed. Reg. 81,839). The proposal is not adopted; in 2026 OPM considers reissuing it and rejects that option as too narrow (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,212).
      2. March 5, 2026OPM proposes the current rule (91 Fed. Reg. 10,904) and later receives 721 unique comments.
      3. July 7, 2026OPM publishes the performance appraisal rule, which takes effect August 6, 2026, and authorizes OPM to set a standardized distribution of ratings (91 Fed. Reg. 41,521).
      4. August 3, 2026The final RIF rule and the RIF appeals rule are published. For RIF notices issued on or after September 2, appeals move from the MSPB to OPM.
      5. August 24, 2026Unions sue OPM over the appraisal and suitability rules; the complaint cites the RIF rule only as context.
      6. September 2, 2026The RIF rule and the appeals rule take effect for RIF notices issued on or after this date.
      7. September 21, 2026OPM caps Level 4 and Level 5 ratings at a combined 40 percent for fiscal year 2026, “a ceiling, not a target,” for career Senior Executive Service members, career senior professionals, and career and career-conditional General Schedule employees. Wage Grade employees, offices of inspectors general, and some other groups are excluded, and bargaining agreements that bar forced distributions remain in force until they expire (OPM 2026c, 2-4).
      8. November 18, 2026A rule on administrative leave, including leave during a RIF notice period, takes effect (91 Fed. Reg. 65,924).
      9. January 1, 2027Agencies must use the new summary-level rating patterns (91 Fed. Reg. 41,521).

      What ARA will watch

      Litigation over the rule. A facial challenge to the rule or the appeals rule, or a VEOA appeal raising § 3502(c), would test the central legal question and the reach of the bar on judicial review.

      Rating distributions. OPM’s fiscal year 2026 results, the cap it sets for fiscal year 2027, and agencies’ choice of summary patterns will set the practical value of the credit.

      RIF volume and composition. Data on separations by preference status would show whether the modeled effects appear in practice.

      DoD alignment. Whether DoD revises its instruction to conform to the new part 351 will determine how its roughly 278,000 preference-eligible veterans are ranked.

      Congressional action. Appropriations riders or legislation on retention preference or RIF appeal rights would change the legal baseline.

      OPM appeal decisions. The first OPM decisions in RIF appeals brought by preference eligibles will show how OPM applies § 3502(c).

      If you have veterans’ preference

      1. Check your ratings of record.

        Confirm that all of your ratings from the past four years appear in your personnel records. Missing ratings are filled in by formula, and your three most recent ratings now set most of your standing.

      2. Check your preference status.

        Confirm that your records show the correct veterans’ preference. Only a compensable service-connected disability of 30 percent or more earns the 5-point credit; other preference eligibles receive 3. A military retiree counts as a preference eligible in a RIF only under the conditions in 5 C.F.R. § 351.504(b).

      3. Know where appeals go.

        For RIF notices issued on or after September 2, 2026, appeals go to OPM rather than the MSPB, and OPM’s rule bars grievances over RIFs and court review of its decisions (91 Fed. Reg. at 49,230, 49,242-43).

      4. Know the complaint deadline.

        A preference eligible who believes a RIF violated a veterans’ preference law or regulation may file a complaint with the Secretary of Labor within 60 days after the date of the alleged violation. If the Secretary has not resolved the complaint within 60 days of filing, the complainant may appeal to the MSPB, no later than 15 days after receiving the Secretary’s written notice of the results. Once an MSPB appeal has been pending for 120 days without a decision on the merits, the complainant may instead file suit in federal district court (5 U.S.C. §§ 3330a(a), (d), 3330b).
